# 1. Why this document exists

Lisa Collio is a licensed real estate agent, not a mortgage lender or loan originator. RESPA (the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act) and its implementing rule, Regulation X, primarily regulate lenders and mortgage servicers — most of Regulation X (escrow accounting, loss mitigation, servicing transfers, force-placed insurance) does not apply to a real estate agent's website and has been deliberately left out of this reference. What does apply to Lisa, and what this document covers, is narrower:

- RESPA Section 8 — the prohibition on kickbacks, referral fees, and unearned fees for referring settlement service business (lenders, title companies, etc.). This governs how Lisa can talk about, link to, or recommend lenders and other settlement providers, and how she runs day-to-day business practices like open houses, co-marketing, and vendor relationships.

- NAR's marketing guidance on RESPA compliance for websites, social media, and co-marketing — directly relevant to blog content, testimonials, and any joint content with a lender.

- Lisa's own stricter rules: no interest rate references anywhere on the site, and lending information sourced only from verifiable, reputable sources (CFPB, HUD, Fannie Mae) — paraphrased, never copied, and cited.

This document is a plain-language operating reference for content and code decisions. It is not legal advice. For anything ambiguous — a new landing page concept, a lender co-marketing idea, a paid lead-gen partnership, a vendor gift, a sponsored event — Lisa should confirm with a RESPA attorney or her broker's compliance contact before publishing or accepting.

# 2. Locked rule: Agent, not lender

Every page, article, or widget on the site that touches financing, affordability, pre-approval, or "working with a lender" must include a clear, plain-language statement that Lisa Collio is a real estate agent — not a lender, loan officer, or mortgage broker — and does not provide loan approvals, underwriting decisions, or financing.

## Required language pattern (use consistently, adapt to context)

*"Lisa Collio is a licensed real estate agent and is not a mortgage lender, loan officer, or broker. She does not originate loans, quote loan terms, or make lending decisions. For financing questions, speak directly with a licensed lender."*

## Where this is required

- Any page or article mentioning financing, pre-approval, down payments, closing costs, or "working with a lender."

- Any lead-capture form that asks about financing needs or offers to "connect you with a lender."

- The site-wide footer (in addition to, not instead of, the page-specific statement on financing content).

- Any contact page, About page, or bio content that could be read as offering lending services.

# 3. Locked rule: No interest rate references — anywhere

This is a strict, no-exceptions rule for Claude Code and for content generation:

| **Absolute prohibition** The website may never state, estimate, imply, compare, or reference an interest rate — current, historical, projected, "around," "as low as," or otherwise — for any loan product, in any format: page copy, blog articles, FAQs, calculators, widgets, alt text, or metadata. This applies even when citing a third-party source (e.g., "rates are currently around X%, according to Freddie Mac" is NOT permitted). This applies even in hypothetical or illustrative examples ("imagine a 6% rate" is NOT permitted). |
| --- |

Why: Lisa is not a lender and has no authority to quote, imply, or represent loan pricing. Rate quoting is a lending function and any rate-adjacent language on an agent's site risks blurring that line and creating consumer confusion about who is originating the loan.

## What's still fine to say

- General, non-numeric statements like "monthly payments depend on your loan terms, which a lender can walk you through."

- Directing the reader to a lender or a neutral calculator tool hosted elsewhere, without restating or summarizing any rate figures from it.

- Discussing affordability, budgeting, and the buying process in general terms, without numeric rate figures.

# 4. Locked rule: Sourcing lending information

When the site needs to explain a lending concept (pre-approval, loan types, the closing process, escrow, PMI, etc.), the information must:

- Come from a small set of pre-approved, verifiable sources: Fannie Mae (fanniemae.com), Freddie Mac, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (consumerfinance.gov), and HUD (hud.gov). These are neutral, non-promotional government or GSE sources — not a specific lender's marketing site.

- Be written entirely in original language — a rewrite in Lisa's voice, not a close paraphrase that mirrors the source's sentence structure or wording.

- Never quote source text directly, even in short phrases, and never reproduce a source's examples, figures, or structure closely enough to count as reproduction rather than explanation.

- Include a clear, visible citation/attribution line naming the source (e.g., "Source: Consumer Financial Protection Bureau") with a link, so the consumer can verify the information independently.

- Avoid citing an individual lender, mortgage company, or loan officer as a source or authority anywhere on the site — this could be read as an endorsement or a referral relationship requiring disclosure under RESPA Section 8 (see Section 5).

If Claude Code cannot verify a lending fact against one of these sources, it should flag the content for Lisa's review rather than publish it from general knowledge — lending rules and figures change and an unverifiable claim is a liability risk.

# 5. RESPA Section 8 — kickbacks, referral fees, and "things of value"

This is the part of RESPA that actually governs a real estate agent's business practices, and it applies beyond the website — to any co-marketing, social media activity, vendor relationship, or referral relationship with a lender, title company, or other settlement service provider.

## The core rule

No one may give or accept a fee, kickback, or "thing of value" in exchange for referring settlement service business (a lender, title company, escrow company, etc.). This applies even to non-cash benefits, and there is no minimum dollar exception.

## What counts as a "thing of value" (broader than most agents assume)

HUD's rules treat almost any benefit tied to a referral as a "thing of value," including things that don't look like a fee. Examples relevant to a real estate agent's day-to-day business:

- Cash, discounts, or waived/reduced fees for services

- Free or below-market advertising, marketing materials, or co-marketing costs

- Sponsored open-house food, drinks, or events paid for by a settlement provider

- Trips, event tickets, gift cards, or payment of expenses

- Equipment, office space, or staff time provided at no or reduced cost

- Contest prizes tied to referral volume

- Increased or reciprocal social media promotion tied to referrals

The test isn't the dollar amount — it's whether the benefit is connected, even informally or by a repeated pattern, to referring business. A pattern of accepting the same perk from the same provider can itself imply an "agreement or understanding" even if nothing was ever said or signed.

## What this means for the website and marketing content

- Do not label any lender, title company, or other settlement provider as a "preferred partner," "preferred lender," or similar endorsement-style term, unless a proper Affiliated Business Arrangement Disclosure is in place and reviewed by Lisa's broker/attorney.

- Do not create the impression of an exclusive or reciprocal relationship with a specific lender (e.g., "work with our trusted lender") without that same disclosure.

- If any co-marketed content, joint social post, or joint landing page with a lender or title company is ever built, it must clearly say "Advertisement," and each party must be paying its own fair-market-value share — this is a business/legal matter for Lisa to confirm, not something the website copy alone can fix.

- General, neutral educational content about how financing works (sourced per Section 4) is fine. Content that steers a reader toward one specific provider is not, unless disclosed.

## Exceptions to the anti-kickback rule — and why most don't help Lisa

RESPA carves out a short list of arrangements that are exempt from Section 8. Two are worth knowing well; the rest are unlikely to apply to Lisa's business but are listed for completeness:

- **Payments for services actually rendered.** The exception Lisa is most likely to rely on. Any payment received (or paid) for a settlement-related service must correspond to real, necessary, distinct work — and the amount must be reasonably related to the value of that work. Simply passing along a lead, filling out a form, or making an introduction is not a compensable service under this exception.

- **Affiliated Business Arrangements (AfBAs).** Two settlement-service providers (e.g., a brokerage and a title company) may co-own a joint venture and share in its profits, but only if a written disclosure is given to the customer before the referral, the customer isn't required to use the affiliated provider, and payments are limited to a genuine return on ownership — never scaled to the volume of business referred. This is the exception directly relevant to the "mortgage partner" flag below.

- **Employer-to-employee payments.** Does NOT apply to Lisa's relationship with her own real estate agents or team members if they are engaged as independent contractors rather than W-2 employees — which is the norm in real estate. A per-referral bonus paid to an independent-contractor agent is not protected by this exception.

- **Other narrower exceptions** (cooperative commission splits between listing/selling brokers acting in a brokerage capacity, payments to attorneys for services rendered, a title company paying its own appointed title agent, and normal non-conditioned promotional items like branded pens or calendars) generally sit outside a real estate agent's typical financing-content decisions but are noted here for completeness.

## Enforcement — and an important update

Historically, HUD investigated and penalized Section 8 violations, and penalties could include civil and criminal fines, repayment of up to three times the referral fee involved, and in serious cases imprisonment. Consumers, state regulators, and the government could all bring an action.

| **Flag for Lisa — enforcement authority has shifted since the source material was written** The source guide this section draws from is dated 2005. Since then, the Dodd-Frank Act (2010–11) transferred primary RESPA rulemaking and enforcement authority to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB); HUD retains a narrower role. Specific penalty amounts and procedures should not be treated as current — this document should flag Section 8 risk in plain language and defer specifics to Lisa's broker or a RESPA attorney, not cite dollar figures or penalty details as current law. |

# 6. Additional topics to avoid or handle carefully

Beyond your two locked rules, these are RESPA-adjacent risk areas that a real estate agent's website and business commonly run into:

- Specific closing cost dollar figures or ranges — these vary by lender and transaction; general "talk to your lender about closing costs" language is safer than a number or range.

- Loan qualification advice — statements like "you'll likely qualify for X" or debt-to-income guidance are lending judgments, not real estate agent guidance.

- Guaranteeing or implying loan approval, timelines, or outcomes ("get pre-approved in 24 hours," "guaranteed financing") — this is lending territory and also raises separate advertising-accuracy issues.

- Using "we" language that blurs Lisa's role with a lender's ("we can get you financed") — keep the agent role and the lending role visually and linguistically distinct.

- Endorsement-style language for any single settlement provider (title company, inspector, lender) without the appropriate disclosure — see Section 5.

- Testimonials or reviews that mention specific loan terms, rates, or a specific lender by name in a promotional way — these should be handled per Lisa's locked reviews rule (verbatim, unaltered) but reviewed for any rate or lender-endorsement content before publishing, since a client's own words could still create a rate-reference or informal-endorsement problem on the site.

- Accepting a settlement provider's sponsorship of an open house, client event, or marketing cost — even food, drinks, or a venue — when it's tied (even informally) to referring that provider business.

- Joint advertising with a lender or title company where each side isn't paying its fair, proportional share of the cost.

- Contests, prizes, or bonuses tied to how much business is referred to a particular lender or provider.

# 7. Real-world scenarios (illustrative, not client-facing)

These plain-language scenarios are adapted from NAR's RESPA compliance guide. They're for Lisa's and Claude Code's internal reference when making judgment calls about business practices and content — not for publishing on the site.

| **Scenario** | **Generally OK?** | **Why it matters for Lisa** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A title company covers the cost of food for an open house Lisa hosts for other agents. | No | Covering the cost is a thing of value tied to referrals unless the provider is there marketing its own services independently, not just funding the event. |
| Lisa and a lender split a newspaper ad, each paying for and using exactly their own half. | Yes | Proportional, fair-market-value joint advertising is allowed; one side subsidizing the other is not. |
| A title company owner buys Lisa dinner while discussing general future marketing ideas, with no referral tied to the meal. | Yes | Ordinary business-relationship courtesies aren't a violation as long as they aren't payment for a referral. |
| Lisa marks up a courier or vendor fee and pockets the difference without doing extra work. | No | Marking up a pass-through fee without added service is treated as an unearned fee. |
| A lender offers a prize (e.g., a trip) to whichever agent sends them the most referrals. | No | A prize tied to referral volume is a textbook kickback regardless of its form. |
| Lisa refers clients to an affiliated title company she has an ownership stake in, and gives clients a written disclosure of the relationship, estimated charges, and their right to choose another provider. | Yes, if properly disclosed | This is the Affiliated Business Arrangement safe harbor — disclosure and free choice are what make it lawful. See the mortgage-partner flag above. |
| An insurance company gives Lisa branded pens, notepads, or a calendar, with no referral condition attached. | Yes | Ordinary, non-conditioned promotional items are treated differently from referral payments. |
| A brokerage pays its independent-contractor agents a flat bonus for every client referred to its affiliated mortgage company. | No | The employer–employee exception doesn't cover independent contractors, which is how most real estate agents are classified. |

# 8. Key terms, in plain language

- **Thing of value:** Virtually any payment, discount, service, gift, or benefit — not just cash. If it has worth and is connected to a referral, it counts.

- **Referral:** Any word or action that steers a consumer toward a particular settlement-service provider, when that consumer will end up paying for the service.

- **Agreement or understanding:** Doesn't need to be written or spoken — a repeated pattern of accepting a benefit tied to referrals can count as one.

- **Required use:** Forcing or effectively pressuring a client to use one specific provider to access some other service or benefit — generally not allowed; any bundled discount must be genuinely optional.

- **Federally related mortgage loan:** In practice, almost any residential purchase-money mortgage on a 1–4 family home — this is the type of loan RESPA's referral rules are built around.

- **Affiliated Business Arrangement (AfBA):** A settlement-service business partly owned by someone (or their associate) in a position to refer to it — allowed only with proper disclosure and no referral-based payments.
