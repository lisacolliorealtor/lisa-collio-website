---

Goshen surprises people. It's a city of about 34,000 — the Maple City, established 1831 — which usually predicts a short list. The honest reality is that the list here is longer than it should be for the population, mostly because the downtown never got hollowed out the way a lot of Midwestern downtowns did. In 2017 the Indiana Chamber of Commerce named Goshen the state's Community of the Year, and the reasons are mostly visible on foot.

Here's what's actually worth doing, organized roughly by how much time you have.

## Downtown, if you have an afternoon

Goshen's Historic District was established in 1983 and runs about 45 city blocks. The storefronts along Main Street date to the late 1800s and early 1900s and are still in commercial use — not preserved as a museum, just still working. Parking downtown is free, and the city publishes an interactive map of the lots.

Independent retail anchors it:

- **The Nut Shoppe** — downtown more than 25 years, hand-dipped chocolates, homemade caramels, turtles
- **Olympia Candy Kitchen** — an old-fashioned candy store attached to one of the more intact vintage diner counters left in the region; the breakfast is the local secret
- **Fables Books** — used books, the kind of store you lose an hour in
- **Found** and **Ten Thousand Villages** — handcrafted goods sourced internationally; Found also carries vintage lighting and antiques
- **The Soapy Gnome** — makes its soaps on site, and you can usually watch
- **Woldruff's Footwear**, **Jules Boutique**, and **Twice as Nice** — shoes, new clothing, and high-end consignment
- **Maple City Market** — a locally owned grocery co-op
- **Flowers by Phoebe** — arrangements from blooms grown on their own small urban farm
- Several thrift stores, including **The Depot**, which is genuinely large

Downtown also operates as a **Designated Outdoor Refreshment Area (DORA)**, which allows open containers from participating businesses inside a defined boundary. On a summer evening that changes the whole feel of the district.

Six Goshen properties are on the National Register of Historic Places: the Goshen Historic District itself, the **Elkhart County Courthouse** at its center, the **Fort Wayne Street Bridge**, the **Goshen Carnegie Public Library**, the **William N. Violett House**, and the **Violett-Martin House and Gardens**. An hour spent finding them is a better architectural tour than most cities this size can offer.

The **Goshen Public Library** and the **Goshen Historical Society museum** — housed in one of the city's original store buildings, wheelchair accessible, about an hour to see — are both downtown and both free. While you're there, look for the small police booth built during the John Dillinger era, back when a bank in a town this size had reason to be nervous.

## The Old Bag Factory

North of downtown, a former industrial building has been converted into working studios and shops. Swartzendruber Hardwood Creations and John Mishler Sculpture Studio are the best-known tenants, alongside makers producing quilts, ironware, stained glass, soaps, and baked goods.

The distinction that matters: these are working studios, not a gift shop with a theme. You can usually watch someone building the thing you're about to buy, which is a different experience than retail.

## Goshen Theater

The **Goshen Theater** at 216 S. Main Street opened in 1905 as the Jefferson Theatre, burned down a year later, was rebuilt in 1907, spent decades as a movie house, closed in the 1980s, and served as a church for nearly thirty years before a nonprofit bought it in 2014 and restored it.

It's now a 600-seat venue programming live music, theater, dance, film, and community events. Worth knowing: the theater offers assistive listening devices, sensory backpacks with noise-cancelling headphones and fidget tools, and a range of seating options — all free to check out at the box office.

**New World Arts** also produces downtown, with a range that runs from musicals to drama.

## Where to eat

Goshen punches considerably above its weight here, and two places have national footprints.

**Venturi** makes Neapolitan pizza in a converted downtown space — readers of *Esquire* once named it among the most memorable pizzas in the country. **South Side Soda Shop** has been visited by Guy Fieri for Food Network's *Diners, Drive-Ins and Dives*; the pies are the reason, though the sandwiches hold up.

Beyond those:

- **Goshen Brewing Company** — house beer, a serious kitchen, and live music
- **Which Craft Brewery** — the other local option
- **Maple Indian** — downtown, with an affordable buffet at lunch
- **Miso Japan** — sushi and Japanese; expect a wait on weekends
- **Noodlelicious** — pho and Vietnamese
- **Anna's Bread** — house-made breads and pastries next to the Farmers Market, plus full meals
- A deep bench of Mexican food, which reflects the city honestly: **Universal Tamal**, **Los Primos Mexican Grill**, and **Tacos El Portal**, a permanent taco truck parked north of downtown on Pike Street
- **Jojo's Pretzels** and **Shirley's Popcorn** for the in-between hours

## Coffee and dessert

**The Electric Brew** on East Washington Street is the one locals name first — an open, noisy, everyone-goes-there workspace. **Embassy Coffee Co.** roasts and pours single-origin coffee with juices and smoothies alongside.

For dessert, **The Chief** has been ranked among the better ice cream shops in the country and is the summer default. Ask about blue moon — it's a Midwest thing, and it's better than it sounds. **Honey's** rotates frozen yogurt flavors constantly.

## Outdoors

The **Pumpkinvine Nature Trail** is the headline — a paved rail-trail running northeast out of Goshen, built on a former rail corridor, with connecting routes linking Goshen, Elkhart, and Shipshewana.

Inside the city, **Fidler Pond Park** has water, paths, and seasonal pedal boat, kayak, and canoe rentals. **Abshire Park** sits along the Elkhart River, and **Water Tower Park**, **Hay Park**, and **Mullet Park** fill in elsewhere. There's a skatepark, plus two dog parks — **Robert L. Nelson** and **Sprocket**.

The **Millrace**, Goshen's historic canal, has a path alongside it that feeds into the **Maple City Greenway**, the city's connected trail network. **Ox Bow Park**, a county park a short drive north along the river, adds trails and a disc golf course.

Seasonally, **Kercher's Sunrise Orchard** draws people out for apples, and **Stutsman Ballooning** operates hot air balloon flights over the countryside.

## Ordinary evenings

Not everything needs to be an occasion. **Linway Plaza** on Lincoln Avenue, just west of downtown, holds a ten-screen cinema with stadium seating and a cluster of restaurants — American, Chinese, Italian, Mexican. There's laser tag and an arcade in town. **GoDance Studio** runs West Coast swing, Latin, and ballroom nights with a lesson beforehand. **Spacious Heart Yoga** covers the other end of the evening.

The **Goshen Farmers Market** runs on a regular weekly schedule and is a fixture rather than a novelty. Check the market's own calendar for current days and hours.

## A little local trivia

Goshen is pronounced GOH-shen, and it takes its name from the biblical Land of Goshen. The 2005 film *Lonesome Jim* — directed by Steve Buscemi, starring Liv Tyler and Casey Affleck — was written by a former Goshen resident and shot here. The band Lotus formed at Goshen College. Film director Howard Hawks, NFL quarterback Rick Mirer, and CNN anchor Kate Bolduan all came from here.

## Something genuinely unusual

**Janus Motorcycles** hand-builds small-displacement motorcycles in Goshen and ships them across the country. A boutique motorcycle manufacturer is not what most people expect to find in a city this size, and it says something accurate about the place — Goshen has a persistent streak of people making physical things well, from the studios at the Old Bag Factory to the RV plants down the road.

## Day trips from Goshen

Almost everything below is inside half an hour.

**Elkhart**, about ten miles north, has **Wellfield Botanic Gardens**, the **RV/MH Hall of Fame and Museum**, **Ruthmere** (a Beaux-Arts mansion museum), and **Linton's Enchanted Gardens**. The county's **Heritage Trail** driving tour connects the towns, and the **Quilt Gardens** — large quilt-pattern flower plantings installed across the county each summer — are genuinely unusual and free to see.

**Shipshewana**, about fifteen miles east, is known for its flea market, specialty shops, the **Blue Gate Theatre**, and **Menno-Hof**, a visitor center interpreting Mennonite and Amish history. **Das Dutchman Essenhaus** in Middlebury is one of Indiana's larger restaurants and a long-standing regional draw. Guided buggy and farm tours operate throughout the surrounding countryside, and a country drive here routinely turns up small home businesses selling baked goods, quilts, and furniture.

Further out: **South Bend**, about 23 miles northwest, has the **University of Notre Dame**, the **Studebaker National Museum**, and the **Potawatomi Zoo**. Chicago is roughly two and a half hours by car — worth remembering that Goshen is on Eastern time and Chicago is on Central.

## One suggestion, from an agent

If you're evaluating Goshen rather than just visiting it, come on a first Friday of the month. It's the one evening where the city is unmistakably itself — the whole downtown is out, the storefronts are open late, and you get an honest read in three hours that would otherwise take three visits.

More than one buyer relocating from a larger metro has arrived expecting an uneventful Friday night and left having changed their mind about the whole town. What's actually happening in that moment isn't the event — it's that they're seeing the place function, which is impossible to get from listing photos.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is Goshen, Indiana best known for?**
Its historic downtown business district, the Old Bag Factory, the Pumpkinvine Nature Trail, and its place in the RV manufacturing corridor.

**Is there much to do in Goshen year-round?**
Yes — First Fridays runs every month regardless of season, the Goshen Theater programs through the winter, and the Elkhart County 4-H Fair anchors the summer. See the full [year-round events calendar](/blog/community/community-events-in-goshen-indiana/).

**How far is Goshen from Chicago?**
About two and a half hours by car via the Indiana Toll Road. Goshen observes Eastern time and Chicago is on Central.

One of the more useful things you can know about a place before you move there is what its calendar looks like. A town with a real calendar behaves differently from one without.

Goshen has a real one. Here's the year.

## Every month: First Fridays

The first Friday of every month, downtown Goshen closes ranks around a coordinated evening event organized by Downtown Goshen, Inc., which launched the program in 2007. Stores stay open until nine. Storefronts stay open late, and the evening carries concerts, classes, promotions, vendors, and whatever the season calls for. The Goshen Theater participates. So does most of Main Street.

It runs year-round — including February, which tells you something about how seriously the city takes it.

For anyone deciding whether Goshen fits, this is the single highest-information evening available. Three hours downtown on a first Friday will tell you more than a weekend of driving around.

## Weekly: the Goshen Farmers Market

The Farmers Market runs on a regular weekly schedule through the year and is a genuine fixture — produce, baked goods, artisan goods, and prepared food from growers and makers in the surrounding area, most of whom you'll see again the following week. Check the market's own calendar for current days and hours.

## Spring: the Riverbend Film Festival

Goshen hosts an annual independent film festival, which is not something most cities of 34,000 can say. It's a good early read on how seriously the city takes its arts scene.

## Summer: the Quilt Gardens

Each summer, large flower plantings arranged in quilt patterns are installed across the county along the Heritage Trail, several of them within a short drive of Goshen. They're free, they're outdoors, and they're the kind of regional tradition that turns out to be a bigger deal than it sounds.

## Also annual: the Goshen Air Show

Goshen Municipal Airport hosts an annual air show — another thing a city of 34,000 has no particular business having, and another reason the airport sits closer to the center of local life here than most municipal fields do.

## Late July: the Elkhart County 4-H Fair

The big one. The Elkhart County 4-H Fair is among the top fifty fairs and expositions in North America, the largest attended county fair in Indiana, and the fourth-largest attraction in the state by attendance. It runs in July.

It's held at the Elkhart County Fairgrounds, inside the city, in late July. Free grandstand concerts have historically drawn nationally touring artists. Beyond the concerts, it's a full agricultural fair — livestock, 4-H project judging, competitions, food, and midway.

If you're relocating with any interest in local life, this is the week the wider area shows up in one place.

## The rest of the year at the fairgrounds

The Elkhart County 4-H Fairgrounds doesn't sit idle for the other eleven months. Through the year it hosts a rodeo, arts and crafts shows, auto shows, auctions, travel rallies, corporate events, and year-round public camping. It's one of the more heavily used venues in the area, which is easy to miss if you only think of it as a fairground.

## Fall and winter downtown

The city carries the calendar into the colder months. Seasonal and holiday programming runs downtown, the Goshen Theater keeps a winter schedule of music, film, and stage productions, and the Goshen Public Library programs consistently year-round.

Goshen College adds a layer most cities this size don't have — public lectures and concerts open to the community, running on the academic calendar rather than the tourist one.

## Where to check what's actually happening

Event dates shift year to year, and a stale calendar is worse than none. The reliable sources:

- **The City of Goshen's community calendar** — the broadest official listing
- **Downtown Goshen, Inc.** — First Fridays and downtown programming
- **The Goshen Chamber of Commerce** — a general community events listing
- **Goshen Theater** — its own performance schedule
- **Goshen Public Library** — year-round programming
- **The Elkhart County Convention & Visitors Bureau** — regional events, including the Quilt Gardens and Heritage Trail

One practical note for summer evenings: downtown operates as a Designated Outdoor Refreshment Area, so participating businesses can serve drinks you're allowed to carry within a marked boundary. It's a small ordinance that makes the monthly events feel considerably more like a block party.

## Why this matters when you're buying

A buyer once told Lisa, six months after closing, that the thing that made Goshen finally feel like home wasn't the house. It was being downtown on a first Friday in December and running into three people they recognized — which, six months earlier, would have been zero.

That's the part that's genuinely hard to evaluate from a distance, and it's why Lisa encourages out-of-state buyers to time at least one visit to an event rather than scheduling purely around showings. You learn different things.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**When is First Fridays in Goshen?**
The first Friday of every month, downtown, year-round. Check Downtown Goshen, Inc. for the evening's specific programming.

**When is the Elkhart County 4-H Fair?**
July. It's the largest attended county fair in Indiana, held at the Elkhart County 4-H Fairgrounds.

**Are there things to do in Goshen in the winter?**
Yes — First Fridays runs through winter, the Goshen Theater programs a full winter schedule, and the library and Goshen College both run year-round events. More in the [things to do guide](/blog/community/things-to-do-in-goshen-indiana/).

The short answer: living in Goshen costs less than the national average, and housing is where most of that difference lives. The longer answer is more useful, because "cheaper" is not the same as "cheap," and the categories that surprise people aren't the ones they budget for.

## Housing

This is the headline and there's no need to be coy about it — housing costs meaningfully less here than in most of the country, and dramatically less than in the metro areas a lot of transplants are leaving.

What it costs *right now* is a different question, and one that changes. Median sale price, days on market, and inventory — broken out by ZIP code — live on the [market data page](/market-stats/), sourced from the local MLS and refreshed quarterly. That's deliberately the only place on this site where price figures appear, so you never get a current number on one page and a two-year-old number on another.

A word of warning about the alternative: relocation articles that quote Goshen home prices without a date attached are worth ignoring entirely. Prices move, and an undated figure tells you when someone wrote an article, not what a house costs.

## Property taxes

Indiana's structure works in a homeowner's favor relative to a number of other states. There's a homestead deduction for owner-occupied properties, and the state constitution caps the property tax rate that can be applied to a homestead — which makes annual carrying costs more predictable than in states where assessments can climb without a ceiling.

Two cautions. First, rates and assessments vary parcel by parcel, so confirm the actual number for a specific address with the county assessor's office rather than working from a percentage rule of thumb. Second, the homestead deduction requires filing — it isn't automatic on a purchase. It's a routine step, but people miss it, and missing it costs real money.

## Utilities

Generally lower than the national average, with one exception that catches nearly every transplant from a warmer state: **winter heating.**

Goshen gets a real winter — snow, extended cold, and lake-effect systems off Lake Michigan. The thirty-year normals are worth seeing before you build a budget: about **41 inches of snow a year**, a **January daily mean near 25°F**, a **July daily mean around 73°F**, and just under **40 inches of annual precipitation** (NOAA 1991–2020 station normals for Goshen, 46526). Heating a drafty older home through January is a genuine line item, and it varies enormously between a well-insulated house and a poorly insulated one.

The practical move: ask for twelve months of utility history on any home you're serious about. Sellers can usually produce it, and the gap between two similar-looking houses can be substantial. It's one of the most useful and least-requested pieces of information in a transaction.

## Transportation

Lower than most places, for a simple reason: the drives are short. Commutes within Goshen are brief, and even a commute to Elkhart, Middlebury, or Mishawaka is manageable without the traffic that makes a comparable distance painful in a metro area.

The offset is that public transportation is limited. The Interurban Trolley connects Goshen to Elkhart and Dunlap and links onward toward South Bend, but it has few stops. Having a car here will prove very useful, and most households run two. Budget accordingly.

## Everything else

Groceries, healthcare, and general services track at or below national averages. Goshen has both independent grocers and national chains, and IU Health Goshen Hospital means primary and specialty care is local rather than a drive away.

## The expense people actually underestimate

Not any single category — the **transition itself.** Moving costs, deposits, immediate repairs, the furniture that doesn't fit the new place, and the two or three months where you're paying for the old life and the new one at once.

Lisa has watched more than one buyer stretch to the very top of their approval amount and then spend the first year uncomfortable, not because the payment was wrong but because there was nothing left behind it. The strongest position isn't the biggest house you qualify for. It's the house that leaves room.

Most people move in the wrong order. Not badly wrong — just wrong enough that they lose a house they wanted, or spend a scouting trip gathering information they could have gotten from a phone call.

Here's the sequence that actually works, roughly three to six months out.

## Step 1: The financing conversation — before anything else

Before you look at a single listing, talk to a licensed lender and find out what you're actually working with.

This is first for a reason that isn't obvious: an out-of-state buyer without financing in hand is at a real disadvantage in this market. Homes here can move quickly, and a seller weighing two offers will take the one that looks certain. Arriving with financing already sorted is the difference between competing and watching.

*Lisa Collio is a licensed real estate agent and is not a mortgage lender, loan officer, or broker. She does not originate loans, quote loan terms, or make lending decisions. For financing questions, speak directly with a licensed lender.*

## Step 2: Get honest about the commute

If you're moving for a job, find out exactly where you'll be reporting — the specific facility, not the company. The RV industry has operations scattered across Goshen, Elkhart, Middlebury, and beyond, and the difference between a twelve-minute commute and a thirty-five-minute one will shape your daily life more than any feature of the house.

Drive it if you can. If you can't, ask someone who does.

## Step 3: Narrow the geography before you fly in

This is the step that saves the most time. Goshen has two ZIP codes — 46526 and 46528 — and beyond the city limits the character changes again toward acreage and country properties.

Have this conversation by phone or video before you book a trip. Lisa does this regularly with out-of-state buyers, and a forty-five-minute call routinely eliminates half the map. That means the scouting visit is spent on real candidates rather than orientation.

## Step 4: The scouting visit

Two to three days, structured:

- **Tour more than one type of housing.** Even if you're certain you want new construction, see one older home. Certainty formed from another state is built entirely out of things you could measure remotely, and it doesn't always survive contact with the actual place.
- **Time it to a first Friday if you can.** It's the highest-information three hours available for reading the town.
- **Drive the commute at the hour you'd actually drive it.**
- **Look at more than the house.** Where you'd buy groceries, where the hospital is, what the street sounds like at seven in the evening.

## Step 5: Making an offer from a distance

Entirely normal here, and it works fine — provided the groundwork is done. What matters: your financing is already in place, you've been in the house or had someone you trust in it, and you've built enough of a relationship with your agent that you can move quickly when something right appears.

Inspections, appraisals, and closing can all be handled remotely with proper coordination. Lisa has managed the whole back half of transactions for buyers who were still three states away.

## Step 6: The Indiana admin nobody tells you about

After closing, a short list that's easy to forget in the chaos:

- **File the homestead deduction** on your property taxes. Not automatic. Costs real money if you miss it.
- **Indiana driver's license and vehicle registration** — there's a deadline after establishing residency; check the BMV for the current window.
- **Voter registration**, if that matters to you.
- **Utilities and internet** — set up before move-in day, not on it.
- **School enrollment**, if applicable, which requires proof of residency.

## The step people skip

Deciding *why* you're moving before deciding *where*.

It sounds soft. It isn't. A buyer relocating for a job with a five-year horizon should be looking at a different house than someone relocating to be near aging parents indefinitely, and those two people routinely walk into the same showing with the same budget and the same list of features.

That's the whole basis of how Lisa works — [The Next Chapter Method™](/next-chapter-method/) starts with the season of life you're in and lets the house serve it, rather than the other way around. From out of state, where you can't rely on instinct about the place, having that clear beforehand matters more, not less.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How far in advance should I start planning a move to Goshen?**
Three to six months is comfortable. Start with the financing conversation, since it determines everything downstream.

**Can I buy a home in Goshen without visiting in person?**

The Goshen Historic District — on the National Register of Historic Places since 1983 — covers roughly 45 city blocks, and the housing near the center of the city runs to the late 1800s and early 1900s. Two individual Goshen houses, the William N. Violett House and the Violett-Martin House and Gardens, carry their own National Register listings. Move outward and you hit mid-century construction. Either way, a lot of the most appealing housing here is old.

That's a genuine strength — the craftsmanship, materials, and lot sizes in these homes are frequently better than anything being built now. But the inspection list is different from a newer house, and buyers coming from markets with newer stock often don't know what to ask.

Here's what Lisa tells them.

## Electrical service

The first thing. Homes from this era have often been rewired once or twice, and the quality of that work varies enormously.

What matters: the size of the service coming into the house, whether the panel is a type that insurers still write on, and whether any original wiring remains in the walls. Some older wiring types are difficult or expensive to insure, and finding that out during underwriting rather than during inspection is a bad surprise.

Ask early. It's a phone call.

## Mechanicals and their age

Furnace, water heater, and air conditioning all have finite lives, and in an older home you frequently inherit at least one that's near the end of it.

This is less about deal-breaking and more about budgeting. A furnace with three years left isn't a reason to walk away — it's a reason to know that a replacement is coming and to plan for it rather than being surprised by it in the second January.

## The roof, and when it was last done

Get the age in writing if possible. Roofs on older homes have often been layered rather than replaced, which shortens the life of the current layer and increases the cost of the eventual tear-off.

## Windows

Original windows in a Goshen home are frequently beautiful and frequently drafty, and in a climate with a real winter that's a live heating-cost question, not an aesthetic one.

There's a genuine tension here. Replacement improves efficiency; it can also strip a lot of what makes the house worth owning. Storm windows and restoration are often the better answer, and they're not always the answer a buyer expects to hear.

## Foundation and drainage

The one Lisa pushes hardest on.

What you're looking for isn't cracks by themselves — most old foundations have some. It's water. Grading that slopes toward the house, downspouts discharging next to the foundation, evidence of past moisture in the basement or crawlspace, and whether whatever caused it was actually fixed or just painted over.

Water is the most expensive problem an older house can have and one of the least visible on a showing. It's also not hypothetical here: in February 2018 the Elkhart River crested at a record 13.2 feet and damaged more than 300 structures in the city. Check the flood map for any specific address — the City of Goshen publishes one — before you get attached to a house.

## Insulation

Older homes were frequently built with little and insulated later, unevenly. It shows up on the utility bill rather than the inspection report, which is why the single most useful document you can request is **twelve months of utility history.** Sellers can usually produce it. Almost nobody asks.

## What to do with all of this

Hire a licensed home inspector. Lisa is not a home inspector and won't pretend to be one — what she does is make sure you know which questions to hand the inspector, and that you get the answers before your inspection period expires rather than after.

For an older home, consider specialist follow-ups where the general inspection raises a flag: an electrician, a structural engineer, or a roofer. It costs a few hundred dollars and it is routinely the best money spent in the entire transaction.

## The thing worth remembering

A buyer once walked into a 1920s house near downtown that they had specifically told Lisa they weren't interested in — they wanted new construction, nothing older than ten years, no projects. They bought it.

The inspection turned up a furnace on its last legs and grading that needed correcting. Neither killed the deal, because both were known before the offer was final and both got priced into the negotiation. That's the actual point of a careful inspection on an old house: not to find a reason to walk away, but to make sure you're buying the house you think you're buying, at a number that reflects it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Are older homes in Goshen a good buy?**
Many are. The materials and construction are often better than current builds, and the inspection is what separates a good one from an expensive one.

**What's the most expensive problem in an older home?**
Water — foundation and drainage issues. It's also one of the hardest things to spot during a showing.

**Should I get a specialist inspection on an older home?**
If the general inspection flags electrical, structural, or roofing concerns, yes. It's inexpensive relative to what it can uncover.
